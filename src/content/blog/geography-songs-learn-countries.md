Ask someone to list the twelve countries of South America and they will usually stall at seven or
eight. Ask them to sing a song they learned at school twenty years ago and the words come back in
order, without effort. That gap is the whole case for **geography songs**: most of geography,
at the start, is learning lists, and a melody is one of the oldest ways people have of keeping a
list in order.

This guide covers why songs work, the geography songs worth knowing, and how to write and record
your own for any region. It finishes with the step most songs skip: getting the names out of your
head and onto a map.

## Why songs help you remember lists

A tune does three jobs that plain words do not. It fixes the **order** of the items, because each
name lands on a particular note. It fixes the **length**, because the rhythm tells you how many
syllables are coming. And it gives you a **cue**: start the first line and the next one follows.

Research backs this up, with a catch. In a 1994 study in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology:
Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, Wanda Wallace found that people recalled a text better when they
had learned it as a song than when they had heard it spoken, but only when the melody was simple
and repeated from verse to verse. A tune that changed from verse to verse took the advantage away.
Twenty years
later, Karen Ludke, Fernanda Ferreira and Katie Overy reported in *Memory & Cognition* that adults
who learned short Hungarian phrases by singing them remembered them better than adults who spoke
them.

Two lessons follow for geography. Keep the melody simple and the same for every verse. And
remember what a song does not do: it helps you recall names in order, but it does not tell you
where those places are. You still need a map for that, which is why the last part of this guide
matters as much as the first.

## Geography songs worth knowing

A few songs have taught whole generations their countries and states.

- **"Yakko's World"** from *Animaniacs* (1993) races through the nations of the world to the tune
  of the "Mexican Hat Dance". It is fun and fast, but it was written in 1993, so some of its names
  are out of date, and it jumps around the map rather than moving in order.
- **"Fifty Nifty United States"** (1961), written by the choral composer Ray Charles (not the soul
  singer), lists all fifty states in alphabetical order and is still a staple of American school
  concerts.
- **Continent and capital songs** made for classrooms are everywhere on video sites, of very mixed
  accuracy. Check any country list against a current map before you learn it; borders and names
  change more often than people expect.

Notice what most of them have in common: they go **alphabetically**. That helps you recite, but it
is no help when you are looking at the map and need to know what sits next to Bolivia. A song you
write yourself can go in map order instead, and that is where it starts to beat the classics.

## How to write your own geography song

You do not need to be musical. You need a region, an order and a tune you already know.

1. **Pick one region, not the world.** Twelve countries is a song; 193 is an opera. Start with
   [South America](/map-quiz/south-america/) (12 countries), [Oceania](/map-quiz/oceania/) (14) or
   Central America (7), and move on when it sticks.
2. **Put the countries in map order.** Sweep across the region the way your eye moves: north to
   south, or clockwise round a big country. When you later look at the map, the song walks you
   across it.
3. **Borrow a tune that is free to use.** "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star", "Yankee Doodle" and
   "Frère Jacques" are all in the public domain and have short, repeating lines, which is exactly
   what Wallace's study says works best.
4. **Count syllables, not words.** "Saint Vincent and the Grenadines" is eight syllables;
   "Chad" is one. Pair long names with short ones so each line fits the melody.
5. **Add a fact to each line if it fits.** "Bolivia and Paraguay have no sea" teaches you the
   names, their neighbours and that they are landlocked, all at once.

Here is a verse for South America, in map order from the north coast down, that fits most simple
tunes:

> Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname across the top,\
> Then Brazil takes half the map before it has to stop.\
> Colombia, Ecuador, and down the coast Peru,\
> Bolivia and Paraguay have no sea to view.\
> Chile is the ribbon and Argentina's wide,\
> Uruguay is little on the River Plate side.

That is all twelve countries, and every line is true: Brazil covers almost half the continent,
Bolivia and Paraguay are its only landlocked countries, and Uruguay sits on the Río de la Plata.
A second verse can do the capitals the same way.

## Turn your lyrics into a song

Once the words are written, there are three ways to make them a song you will actually play.

**Sing it yourself.** The quickest option. Sing the verse to the tune you picked, then record it on
your phone so you can play it on the way to school or work.

**Record it with a class or family.** Teachers often find a group version sticks best, because
everyone has a part. Split the verse so each pupil sings one line, and the whole class learns all
twelve names by hearing them in order.

**Have it produced.** If you would rather hear a finished track, with a singer, a beat and a chorus
that loops, an AI song generator such as [Tunora](https://tunora.io/) can turn pasted lyrics into a
complete song. You describe the style you want, say "upbeat pop song for kids" or "slow acoustic
song for revision", paste your verse, and it sings your words; each request comes back in two
versions, so you can keep the catchier one. Its free plan covers personal use, which is enough for
a homework playlist; check the licence before you use a track in a video you publish.

Whichever route you take, listen for one thing: **pronunciation**. Place names trip up singers and
software alike. If "Suriname" or "Paramaribo" comes out wrong, spell it the way it sounds
("Soo-ri-nahm") in the lyrics and try again. A song that drills the wrong pronunciation is worse
than no song.

## From song to map: make it stick

A song gets the names in. The map is how you find out whether you know them. A routine that works:

1. **Play the song once.** Before you open any map, run through the list in your head.
2. **Explore in Learn mode.** Open the matching [map quiz](/map-quiz/) and tap each country as the
   song names it. The order of the song becomes a path across the map.
3. **Test in Click mode.** The quiz names a country and you find it. Watch which lines of the song
   you still need to sing under your breath; those are the countries you have not learned yet.
4. **Finish in Type mode.** The quiz highlights a country and you type its name. If you can do this
   without the song, you know the region.
5. **Repeat tomorrow, then in a few days.** Use Retry missed at the end of each run so you practise
   only the countries you got wrong.

Songs also pair well with our other games. Once a region is in your head, try connecting two of its
countries over land in [Globle Routes](/routes/), or find a mystery country from distance clues in
[Globle Unlimited](/).

## Song ideas for other regions

Some regions almost write their own songs, because the order on the map is already a sequence:

- **Central America**, north-west to south-east: Guatemala, Belize, El Salvador, Honduras,
  Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Panama. Seven names, one line each.
- **The Lesser Antilles**, north to south: Saint Kitts and Nevis, Antigua and Barbuda, Dominica,
  Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Grenada. A chain of island nations that most
  people never learn; practise them in the [North America map quiz](/map-quiz/north-america/).
- **The seven "-stans"**: Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan,
  Afghanistan and Pakistan. The shared ending does half the rhyming for you. Test them in the
  [Asia map quiz](/map-quiz/asia/).
- **The Gulf**, heading south-east from Iraq: Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Qatar, the United
  Arab Emirates and Oman.
- **The fifty US states** in map order rather than alphabetical order, region by region; the
  [US states map quiz](/map-quiz/us-states/) groups them by Census region to help.

## Common questions

**Do geography songs really work?** For remembering names in order, yes, as long as the melody is
simple and repeats. They do not teach location on their own, so pair them with a map quiz.

**What age are they for?** Any. Young children enjoy them most, but the memory effect in the studies
above was measured in adults.

**Should a song go alphabetically or in map order?** Map order. Alphabetical songs are easier to
write, but map order teaches you which countries are neighbours, which is what quizzes and real
maps ask for.

**How long should a geography song be?** One region per song, and short enough to sing in under a
minute. If a region is big, such as Africa with 54 countries, write one verse per sub-region.
